Revenge Bedtime Procrastination: Why You Stay Up Late When You're Exhausted

By Tamara RoseFounder, The Beyond Human MethodUpdated October 10, 20263 minute read

Do you ever stay up far later than you meant to, scrolling, watching one more episode or simply sitting in the quiet, because it's the only time all day that feels like yours? That habit has a name, revenge bedtime procrastination, and once you understand what's driving it, you can give yourself the time you're craving without trading away your sleep.

What revenge bedtime procrastination is

Bedtime procrastination means going to bed later than you intended, without anything outside you keeping you up, even though you know you'll feel it tomorrow. Researchers at Utrecht University first described it in a 2014 study, which linked it with getting too little sleep. The "revenge" part came later from social media, describing people who stay up to take back the personal time their packed days didn't give them.

Why it happens

A few things tend to drive it. After a long day, most of us have very little willpower left, and the pull of something easy and enjoyable wins. Night owls are more prone to it too; a 2018 diary study found that later chronotypes reported more bedtime procrastination on work days. And for many of us, especially women carrying work, family and the mental load, late night is the first moment no one needs anything, so staying up feels like reclaiming a piece of yourself.

What it costs you

The trade rarely pays off. You already know how a short night feels the next day, and the original bedtime procrastination study linked the habit with getting too little sleep. Late-night screens make it worse; a 2015 Harvard study found that reading on a light-emitting screen before bed delayed melatonin and made it take longer to fall asleep. Over time you end up more depleted, which makes the next day feel even more like it belongs to everyone but you.

How to reclaim your evenings

  • Find your time earlier. Protect twenty to thirty minutes during the day or early evening that are just for you, so you're not starving for it at midnight.
  • Set a wind-down alarm. An alarm an hour before bed tells you it's time to start the transition, not to go to sleep instantly.
  • Charge your phone outside the bedroom. Replace it with a book, music or a warm bath.
  • Make "me time" restful. Choose things that refill you, like reading, stretching or meditation, over things that wind you up.
  • Be curious, not critical. Ask what you're really hungry for when you stay up. The answer often points to something in your day that needs to change.

Common questions

What is revenge bedtime procrastination?

It's going to bed later than you intended, without anything outside you keeping you up, often scrolling or watching TV to reclaim personal time you didn't get during the day, even though you know you'll be tired tomorrow.

Why do I stay up late when I'm tired?

By the end of the day you have little willpower left, night owls are more prone to it, and late night may be the only time that feels like yours. That combination keeps you up even when your body wants sleep.

How do I stop revenge bedtime procrastination?

Give yourself protected personal time earlier in the day, set a wind-down alarm, keep your phone out of the bedroom and fill your evening with restful things you enjoy.
